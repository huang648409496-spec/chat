DIN Rail Interface Terminal Blocks: Complete Guide for RJ45, DB9 and DB25

DIN rail interface terminal blocks convert standard connectors into numbered screw or spring terminals — making control cabinet wiring easier to install, label, test, and maintain. This guide covers RJ45, DB9, and DB25 modules: what they do, where they fit, and how to choose the right one.

TL;DR — Key Takeaways

  • DIN rail interface terminal blocks convert RJ45, DB9, or DB25 connectors into screw/spring terminals for cleaner cabinet wiring.
  • They are passive pass-through modules — they do not change the signal or protocol.
  • Choose based on connector type, pin assignment, wiring method, shielding needs, and certification requirements.
  • Most modules mount on standard 35 mm top-hat DIN rail (IEC/EN 60715).
  • Customization (pin layout, housing, labeling) is available from OEM manufacturers, typically 4–6 week lead time.

Definition: A DIN rail interface terminal block is a modular wiring device that converts a standard connector (RJ45, DB9, or DB25) into screw or spring terminals on a 35 mm DIN rail, allowing easier installation, testing, and maintenance inside control cabinets.

What Is a DIN Rail Interface Terminal Block?

A DIN rail interface terminal block is a passive interface module. On one side it carries a standard connector — RJ45, DB9, DB25, or another D-sub variant. On the other side it presents the same signals as individually numbered screw or spring terminals. The module clips onto a standard 35 mm DIN rail inside a control cabinet.

It is not an Ethernet switch, a signal converter, or a protocol gateway. It does not regenerate signals, isolate grounds, or change voltage levels. Its job is purely mechanical: turn one connector into a row of accessible terminals.

That distinction matters. Engineers sometimes assume an "RJ45 terminal block" must add Ethernet functionality. It does not. The module is electrically transparent — what comes in on the connector goes out on the terminals, pin for pin (or according to a custom mapping if specified).

Why Use Interface Terminal Blocks Instead of Direct Connectors?

Direct connector-to-connector wiring works for short runs and lab setups. In production cabinets built for 5–15 year service life, terminal-based wiring wins on six practical points:

  1. Clear labeling — Each signal sits on a numbered terminal. Service techs find Pin 4 in seconds instead of tracing a cable.
  2. Easier testing — A multimeter or oscilloscope clips directly onto the terminal. No breakout adapter needed.
  3. Faster field replacement — Disconnect by loosening terminals, not by recrimping a connector.
  4. Cleaner cable routing — Field cables enter from one side, jumpers leave from the other. No spaghetti.
  5. Lower long-term cost — One terminal block lasts years; field-installed RJ45 plugs often need recrimping after vibration or temperature cycling.
  6. Documentation that survives — Terminal numbers map 1:1 to electrical drawings, making audits faster.

The trade-off: an extra module in the cable path, a small BOM cost, and 5–10 cm of extra rail space. For any cabinet with a multi-year service life, that trade is almost always worth making.

Three Common Connector Types: RJ45, DB9, DB25

RJ45 Interface Terminal Block

The RJ45 module is the most widely used variant in modern cabinets. It accepts a standard 8P8C plug and breaks the eight pins out to terminals.

Typical uses:

  • Industrial Ethernet — Profinet, EtherNet/IP, Modbus TCP
  • PLC-to-HMI links and controller communication
  • Field device communication — sensors, drives, I/O blocks
  • Custom 8-wire signal wiring in legacy or proprietary equipment

Important: RJ45 is a connector format, not a protocol. The same plug appears on Ethernet, RS-485-over-RJ45 systems, telephone wiring, and proprietary serial buses. Always confirm the device's pinout before wiring.

DB9 Interface Terminal Block

The DB9 (formally DE-9) is a 9-pin D-subminiature connector — the standard physical interface for serial communication ports including RS-232, RS-422, and RS-485.

Typical uses:

  • RS-232 point-to-point serial communication
  • RS-485 multi-drop bus (Modbus RTU, Profibus, BACnet MS/TP)
  • RS-422 full-duplex differential signaling
  • CNC, weighing systems, barcode scanners and legacy automation hardware
  • Service and configuration ports on industrial controllers

The DB9 module's value is in maintenance access. A terminal block lets a technician land an RS-485 bus on screw terminals, attach a 120 Ω termination resistor across A/B, and probe the polarity — all without unscrewing a connector.

DB25 Interface Terminal Block

The DB25 carries 25 pins in a single D-sub housing. It is less common in Ethernet-based systems but essential where high pin counts must travel through one connector.

Typical uses:

  • Multi-axis CNC machine interfaces — limit switches, drive enables, encoder feedback
  • Test and measurement equipment with parallel signal interfaces
  • Legacy parallel ports — older PLCs, Centronics-style printers
  • Custom multi-signal breakout for specialized industrial equipment

DIN Rail Mounting Standards

Most industrial cabinets worldwide use the 35 mm top-hat rail defined in IEC/EN 60715 (previously referenced as DIN EN 50022). This is the default mounting format for interface terminal blocks.

A few less common rail formats exist (G-rail per EN 60715, miniature 15 mm rail), but for general-purpose interface modules, 35 mm top-hat is the safe assumption.

Always confirm before ordering: rail width (35 mm), rail height (7.5 mm or 15 mm), and rail material (steel or stainless for harsh environments).

Typical Applications by Industry

Interface terminal blocks appear wherever a cabinet must terminate a structured connector and route signals to field wiring. The common factor is not the industry — it is the need to maintain, document, and service the cabinet over many years.

  • PLC control cabinets — Communication links between PLCs, HMIs, drives, and remote I/O.
  • CNC machines and machine tools — Service ports, encoder feedback, and operator panel interfaces.
  • Building automation — BACnet, Modbus, and access control wiring inside electrical rooms.
  • Solar inverters and ESS cabinets — Communication between battery management, inverters, and SCADA.
  • Test and measurement panels — Bench-top systems where engineers need easy probe access.
  • Marine and offshore control cabinets — Shielded variants for EMI-heavy environments.
  • AGV/AMR fleet charging stations — Communication and diagnostic interfaces between vehicle and infrastructure.

Installation Notes and Common Mistakes

These six points cover the issues seen most often in field reports. They are also the first checks during troubleshooting.

  1. RJ45 ≠ Ethernet. Confirm the device's pinout before assuming a standard T-568A/B layout. A non-standard RJ45 device will appear "dead" on a normal Ethernet cable.
  2. RS-485 polarity matters. A and B (or D+ and D−) must match across all nodes on the bus. Reversing polarity is the single most common RS-485 wiring error.
  3. Use 120 Ω termination on RS-485. Place one termination resistor at each physical end of the bus — not at every node.
  4. Single-point grounding for shields. Multi-point grounding can create ground loops. In most cabinets, ground the shield at one end only.
  5. Separate power and signal wiring. Maintain at least 100–200 mm between mains wiring and low-voltage signal wiring, or use grounded metal divider strips.
  6. Don't oversize the wire. A 2.5 mm² wire forced into a small terminal can damage the contact spring. Match gauge to terminal specification.

Customization Options

Most projects can use off-the-shelf interface modules. When they cannot — non-standard pinout, special housing, integrated LEDs, or private-label packaging — OEM/ODM customization is the path forward.

Common customizations include:

  • Custom pin assignment — Non-standard mapping between connector pin and terminal number
  • Mixed connector types — RJ45 + DB9 in a single housing
  • Custom housing color or printed labels — For brand consistency or signal identification
  • Integrated LED indicators — Power, link, or status indication
  • Special connector variants — Shielded RJ45, ruggedized D-sub, sealed housings
  • Custom certifications — UL, CSA, PSE, CCC, or regional approvals
  • Private-label packaging — For distributors or system integrators

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a DIN rail interface terminal block?

It is a passive wiring module that converts a standard connector (RJ45, DB9, or DB25) into numbered screw or spring terminals on a 35 mm DIN rail, making cabinet wiring easier to install, label, and service.

Is an RJ45 DIN rail terminal block the same as an Ethernet switch?

No. The terminal block is a passive pass-through — it connects each RJ45 pin directly to a terminal. It does not switch packets, regenerate signals, or perform any active function.

Can DB9 terminal blocks be used for RS-485?

Yes. RS-485 typically uses pins 3 (A/D+), 8 (B/D−), and 5 (GND) on a DB9. Always verify the device's specific pinout, as some manufacturers use non-standard layouts.

What is the difference between DB9 and DB25 terminal blocks?

DB9 modules carry 9 signals — usually serial communication. DB25 modules carry 25 signals for multi-axis machine control, parallel interfaces, or high-pin-count breakouts. Mechanical housings differ accordingly.

Can the pin assignment be customized?

Yes. Most manufacturers can reroute pin-to-terminal mapping, change connector type, integrate LEDs, or modify the housing. Custom layouts usually require a quotation and a 4–6 week lead time.

What DIN rail standard do these modules use?

The standard 35 mm top-hat rail defined in IEC/EN 60715 (formerly DIN EN 50022). This is the most common rail in industrial cabinets worldwide.
